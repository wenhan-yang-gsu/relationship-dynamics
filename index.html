Relationship Dynamics Simulation of a Five-State Markov Model

Wenhan Yang and Zhiyu Liu · Advisor: Professor Yi Jiang

Where is a relationship going?

Two people can have no tie, be friends, have romantic potential, be a couple, or be married. Relationships change in both directions over time: friends drift apart, couples break up, and some couples marry.

We model these five states in the way that a predator–prey or SIR model treats populations. Each arrow is a transition with a rate, the expected number of such transitions per year. We estimated the rates from two longitudinal surveys that cover different stages of a relationship. One followed 208 college freshmen, who reported on new acquaintances every three weeks; the other followed 3,009 American couples from 2009 to 2017.

The traits of a pair shift these rates, because mutual interest, cohabitation, years together and relationship quality each speed up some transitions and slow down others. The Simulation page lets you change these traits and watch the resulting trajectories.

This page draws the same model as stocks and flows. Each box (stock) holds the share of pairs in one state. Each pipe (flow) carries pairs from one box to another at a rate times the box it drains, for example dating = qRP→CR × RP.

A box grows with its inflows and shrinks with its outflows. Unlike a population model, no pairs are added, so the five shares always sum to 100%. The rates follow the traits set on the Simulation page.

The continuous-time Markov model

The rate of the transition from state i to state j depends on the traits x of the pair. Traits are standardized, so 0 corresponds to an average pair. An increase of one standard deviation in trait k multiplies the rate by eβ.

qi→j(x) = exp( αij + Σk βijk xk )

Together, all rates form the generator matrix Q of the Markov chain. Each diagonal entry equals minus the total rate of leaving that state, so every row sums to zero. The state probabilities P(t) satisfy a linear differential equation whose solution is a matrix exponential:

dP/dt = P·Q    ⇒    P(t) = P(0)·eQt

We estimated α and β by maximum likelihood from 13,680 observed transitions, with a penalty λ on β selected by 5-fold cross-validation. A trait enters a transition only when the training data contain enough variation in that trait and enough examples of that transition. This rule retained 113 of the 288 possible trait effects on transitions.
